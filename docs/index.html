Act two // opening the box

The fast loop lives where it belongs.

A companion computer cannot hold an attitude: it sits on the far side of a cable, answering at whatever rate it can manage. So the reflexes live on a board we write the firmware for, and the Pi is left to do nothing but think.

What actually crosses the cable

If the reflexes live on the board, the Pi should have almost nothing to say — and it doesn't. Here is the whole of move_forward, the most ordinary thing the vehicle is ever asked to do, exactly as it leaves the Pi. Read it left to right, the way the board does.

The first ten bytes are the envelope: where a frame starts, how long it is, who sent it, what kind of message it is. The part that means anything is three numbers — a type, a duration, a speed — and one tag that says “this is ours”. There is no thrust in it. No motor, no angle, no depth. Which thrusters spin, how hard, and how the hull holds its heading for those three seconds is the board's business; the Pi only said what it wanted.

What a control loop is, if you have never written one.

A controller is a machine that answers one question, over and over: I am here, I want to be there — how hard do I push? Below is a toy hull with one axis of heading. Turn the three knobs and watch it fail in the three classic ways. Nothing here is our vehicle: it is a simulation with made-up numbers, so that the shapes are honest even though the values are not.

Four things worth meeting by hand. P alone overshoots and rings: nothing is braking it, so at the moment it is finally pointing the right way it is still turning. Add D and the ringing dies, because it now pushes back against its own approach speed. At three seconds a current starts pushing, and the PD controller parks a degree or two short of the target for ever — it is pushing exactly hard enough to hold station and not hard enough to close the last degree. Only I fixes that, by remembering what is still missing. Then move the loop rate: the gains do not change, but the window in which the vehicle is on its own does. That is the number in the fourth readout.

On this vehicle that last knob is not ours to turn from the Pi. It is the board's, and it is pinned at 500 Hz on a core nothing else is allowed on — which is the whole argument of the section above.

A controller is not clever. It is patient, five hundred times a second.

Where the design is strong

Four tunnels and one axial unit buy five of six degrees of freedom — surge, sway, heave, pitch, yaw — with no vectoring linkage, no moving nozzles and nothing protruding into the flow. Tunnels also deliver full authority at zero forward speed, which is precisely the regime a task is won in: holding still in front of a hole. Bilateral symmetry is exact to ±88.05 mm, so there is no built-in turn to trim away. And the pitch couple is 48 % wider than the yaw couple (519 mm against 350 mm), which puts the stronger moment on the axis that has to resist a nose-down trim.

Where it costs

The four bores total 221.7 cm² of open aperture on a hull whose frontal area is 160.9 cm² — the holes come to 1.38× the frontal area. Open tunnels are cavities in cruise: they are the price of station-keeping authority, and it is paid continuously while transiting. At a fineness of 3.99 the body is already blunter than the drag optimum, so the two choices compound. The couples are also arguably the wrong way round — yaw is the axis an AUV actually uses, to hold heading against current and to line up on a target, and yaw got the narrower 350 mm couple. Swapping the tunnel pairs would favour the axis doing the work. And with every thruster on the centreplane, roll has no actuator at all: it has to be passively stable, which promotes the vertical placement of ballast from a detail to a load-bearing decision.

What this drawing cannot tell you

All of the above is geometry, and geometry is half a hull. These are unmeasured, and will not be guessed at here:

  • Net buoyancy — whether it floats at allneeds material densities
  • Centre of buoyancy above centre of mass — the whole roll argumentneeds a mass model
  • Drag coefficient, and so the power to cruiseneeds a tow test or CFD
  • Thrust per tunnel, and so whether five are enoughneeds a bollard test
  • Whether the modelled motors are the ones fitted3 of 5 populated in CAD

A shell mesh cannot answer the buoyancy question, and it would be easy to pretend otherwise: the enclosed volume of this model is 1.77 L, which is the surface and not the displacement. Using it would repeat a mistake this project has already made once, and written down. The geometry here is read straight out of the CAD by pack_hull.py, which is also what builds the model above.

Every number on this hull was read off the geometry. The ones that could not be read — mass, buoyancy, drag — are left blank, not guessed.

Play with it // when the vehicle cannot do what it was asked

Ask for more thrust than exists, and the vehicle does something you never asked for.

"Forward, a little left, and turn" is three numbers. The vehicle has four horizontal thrusters, each of which can only give so much. When the sum of those demands exceeds what a motor can deliver, something has to give — and the choice of what gives is a design decision most people never make on purpose. Make the wrong one and a vehicle asked to go straight quietly turns instead.

motors saturated—
you actually get—
heading error—

The arithmetic, in full

Four horizontal units at 45°, so each one contributes to all three axes at once: m₁ = s + l + y, m₂ = s − l − y, m₃ = s − l + y, m₄ = s + l − y. Ask for 0.8 forward and 0.4 of turn and one motor is already being asked for 1.55 of a thing that stops at 1.0.

Clip it there and the four motors no longer stand in the ratio the demand described. Run the arithmetic backwards — s′ = (m₁+m₂+m₃+m₄)/4 and so on — and the motion that comes out has a yaw term nobody asked for. The vehicle is not disobeying. It is doing exactly what four clipped numbers mean.

Why the shape is the thing to protect

Scaling the whole demand by one factor costs speed and keeps direction: you arrive later, along the line you meant. Clipping costs direction and keeps speed: you arrive somewhere else, on time. For a vehicle following a visual target, the second failure is far worse, because the controller sees the drift and asks for more correction — which saturates harder.

The board scales, and the host fits the demand yaw-first before sending it (allocation.py), on the view that a heading you did not ask for is the most expensive thing to be given.

⚠ One honest caveat: the mixer above is the firmware's eight-thruster geometry, of which four are horizontal. The hull currently in CAD has five thrusters in a different arrangement. Both are true of something; they are not true of the same vehicle, and this figure says which one it is drawing.

Saturation is where a vehicle stops doing what it was told and starts doing arithmetic.

Hengla — what runs on the board

Core 1 is the flight loop and nothing else is permitted to run there: read the IMU, decide what each axis should do, split that across eight thrusters, listen to what came back, repeat two milliseconds later. Core 0 carries everything that can afford to wait — MAVLink at 100 Hz, LoRa and the SD card at 20, the display at 30. The RP2350 speaks bidirectional DShot, so an ESC reports the RPM it actually reached rather than the one it was asked for.

That last detail is the one worth pausing on. Without it the motors never answer: you ask for thrust and the honest answer to "is thruster three actually spinning?" was nobody knows. Telemetry back from the ESC turns an open question into a measurement — which is exactly how we learned that 958 of 958 recorded ESC frames read zero with nothing plugged in, and that counting messages is not a health check.

Bondor — the operator's side

An Electron and React desktop application built specifically for this board: teleop, a SROT_MOVE console, parameters, calibration, motor test, PID tuning and autotune, black-box analysis and a configurable joystick tab. It connects over USB serial at 115200 — opened with DTR and RTS de-asserted, deliberately, so that attaching a ground station does not reset the flight controller — or over UDP, or receive-only over LoRa at 433 MHz, 250 kHz bandwidth, SF7, CR 4:5.

Three copies of the wire contract exist on purpose, because there are no submodules across the suite: the LoRa struct, the vendored MAVLink dialect, and a hand-mirrored TypeScript copy of the message ids. The firmware is the source of truth for all three, and a mismatch does not raise an error — it fails CRC silently and looks exactly like being out of radio range. On our side the same hazard is answered by a test that reads the firmware's own headers and fails if our constants have drifted from them.

Play with it // four switches, one observable

Only the product is visible. Two wrongs look exactly like a right.

Between "turn left" and the water there are four independent places a sign can be flipped: the axis convention the host writes, a firmware parameter that negates every axis before the mixer, a firmware revision that changed what positive yaw means, and the direction each motor was told it spins. You can only ever observe their product — which way the hull actually went. Every one of these has been wrong on this vehicle at least once, and the register of that is in BUGS.md.

Why a passing test is not a correct configuration

Pick "two wrongs" above. The hull turns the way it was asked, every bench check passes, and the vehicle is misconfigured in two places at once. Nothing in the observable disagrees, because the observable is a product and the product is right. The configuration only reveals itself when someone corrects one of the two — at which point a fix makes the vehicle worse, which is the single most confusing thing that can happen to a team on a pool deck.

This is why the rule here is to read the parameters before arming rather than to test the turn. A turn tells you about the product. Only the parameters tell you about the factors.

What the vehicle actually does about it

The firmware revision is a hull-safety interlock: the board reports SROT_FW_BEHAVIOUR_REV, and the host refuses at connect and again inside arm() if it is below the floor in srot_protocol.py. The floor is deliberately not a number written in prose anywhere — a test fails any document that quotes a stale one, because a remembered constant is how a check gets silently disabled.

The other three switches are configuration, and configuration is checked by reading it. FRAME_REVERSE = 1 with MOT_1..8_DIRECTION all −1 is the documented double-inversion hazard: if all eight read −1, the axis fix is cancelled and six of the motors are individually inverted — a hull that turns correctly and mixes incorrectly.

A sign error is the only bug that can be fixed twice and thereby unfixed.

    Play with it // one cable, and what fits down it

    Telemetry is free until it isn't, and then it takes the heartbeat with it.

    Everything the board says crosses one USB-C cable at 115 200 baud — about 11 520 bytes a second once the start and stop bits are paid for. Today the link runs at a measured 18.8 % of that. The temptation, always, is to add "just one more" stream; the cost is not that the new stream is slow, it is that everything already on the wire starts queueing behind it — including the heartbeat the board uses to decide whether anyone is still there.

    What the model is, and what it is not

    Measured: the link's capacity, the 18.8 % the vehicle uses today, and the heartbeat at 2 Hz against the board's 5 s silence failsafe — a 10× margin — and the bar this project ships against, which is ≤ 60 % of the wire. Those are in the ledger with their method.

    Modelled: the queueing delay above. It is the standard result for a link at utilisation ρ — the wait grows like ρ/(1−ρ), which is flat and boring for most of its range and then vertical. The curve is drawn to make that shape felt, not to predict a millisecond. A figure that blurs which half is measured is worse than no figure.

    Why the heartbeat is the thing that breaks

    The board surfaces the vehicle after 5 s without hearing from the Pi. That is a deliberate, good failsafe — and it means the heartbeat is the one message whose lateness is a vehicle-level event rather than a logging nuisance. A saturated wire does not announce itself as "telemetry is slow". It announces itself as a vehicle that surfaced in the middle of a run for no reason anyone can see on deck.

    This is also why the interesting number is not bandwidth but headroom. At 18.8 % the link is not "mostly empty" — it is a system with a known distance to a cliff, and the only honest way to add a stream is to measure the distance again afterwards.

    A link is not full or empty. It is near or far from the point where waiting begins.

    The safety chain // what stops the vehicle, and what does not

    Eight links between a mistake and a hull on the bottom of the pool. One of them is missing.

    A vehicle is not made safe by one clever guard. It is made safe by a chain of dull ones, each owned by a different part of the system, each catching a failure the others cannot see. Pick a failure below and watch which link takes it. The chain is drawn as it is configured today — including the link that is switched off and the one that cannot tell two different states apart.

    Why a chain, and not a better guard

    Each link is simple enough to be obviously right, and each one lives where its failure can actually be seen. The board cannot know a mission is stuck; the host can. The host cannot act if the Pi has frozen; the board can. A human cannot react in two milliseconds; the loop can. No single layer could hold all eight, which is exactly why none of them tries.

    It is also why the order of operations in arm() matters more than any one check in it: the firmware revision is checked again at arm, not only at connect, because a board can be re-flashed between the two and a revision below the floor turns every command backwards.

    The one link we cannot close from here

    The leak link was off, and is on. On 2026-09-07 this board read LEAK_EN = 0 — the firmware gates both the pre-arm refusal and the surface failsafe on that one parameter, so a leak would have done nothing. It was switched on before the bench session on 2026-09-15, and the reading above says so: LEAK comes back present, enabled and healthy. The link is only as good as its last reading, which is why this figure carries the timestamp of one.

    The kill link is ambiguous. KILL = 0 means "live" or "the power board is not talking to us", and the host cannot tell which. So arm() refuses only on a known engaged switch, and the display says UNKNOWN rather than clear when the second board is silent. That is the honest reading, not a fix — the fix is one line of firmware, and it has been asked for.

    Safety is not the guard that catches everything. It is knowing exactly which failure each guard will not see.

    A run, from the inside // what it sees, believes and does

    Ride along inside Mongla's head for one run.

    Three panes, one clock. On the left is the forward camera as the detector sees it. In the middle is what the vehicle believes — its own position, the course it was told about, and how sure it is. On the right is what it decides to do about that. Nothing here is a cartoon of the pipeline: the rates, the detection gaps, the lock ladder and the refusals are the measured ones, and the narration is a sentence the code could actually have produced.

    Why three panes and not one

    Every failure this stack has had lives in the gap between two of these panes. A detector that sees nothing while the belief pane says the gate is dead ahead is a lock problem. A belief that drifts while the camera holds a target steady is an estimator problem. A demand that keeps pushing while both agree the target is centred is a control problem. One pane cannot show you which of the three you are looking at, and a single "it works" video hides all of them.

    This is also the argument for the scorecard: every vision verb records what it saw, where it ended and why it stopped, so a run can be read back in these three columns afterwards instead of being remembered.

    What it is allowed to say

    The narration is deliberately restricted to things the code has a field for: the class it is looking for, whether it saw it inside the recency window, the signed pixel offset, which rung of the lock ladder it is on, the verb in flight, and the outcome — ALIGNED, LOST, TIMEOUT, NO_CAMERA, ABORTED. There is no line in here that the vehicle could not have written itself, because a narration that knows more than the vehicle is a story, not a readout.

    The vehicle is never confused. It is specific about what it does not know — and that is the whole difference.

    Play with it // the lens the water grinds for you

    Water takes a third of the picture, before the first pixel is read.

    A camera's field of view is a property of the lens, and every datasheet states it for air. Put the same camera behind a flat window in water and the number is simply no longer true: rays bend as they cross the glass, the cone narrows, and the edges of the world quietly leave the frame. Nothing warns you. The image is still sharp, still centred, still the same 640 pixels wide — it just contains less world.

    Why the cone narrows

    A flat port is a pane of glass with water on one side and air on the other. A ray arriving from the water at angle θw leaves into the air at θa, and Snell's law fixes the relationship: nw sin θw = sin θa. The sensor still accepts rays out to its own half-angle, so the half-angle it accepts in the water is asin(sin θa / nw) — always smaller. The cone does not move or blur; it shrinks.

    This is also why the picture is not simply cropped. The bending is angle-dependent, so a straight line near the edge comes back curved, and a pixel's bearing is not the bearing the pinhole model would give it. That correction lives in optics.py, and it is applied before any pixel becomes a direction.

    What we measured, and what it cost

    The datasheet says 63.8°. In water we measured 46.7° — and Snell, from the index of water alone, predicts 46.71°. Two independent routes to the same number is the strongest form of "this is right" available on a bench.

    The cost of having believed the datasheet is not a blurry picture. It is every bearing being wrong by a growing amount toward the edges, every range estimate built on that bearing being wrong with it, and a standoff distance chosen so that a prop fits — computed against a cone 37 % wider than the one the vehicle actually has.

    A number measured in air is not a number about the vehicle. It is a number about the lab.

It was measured in air, on a hand slide whose own precision is about ±1 cm: the operator's tape is inside our error bar, so it is an upper bound on the error, not a measurement of it.

    Play with it // the correction that arrives from the past

    Every measurement is a statement about a moment that has already gone.

    A camera exposes a frame, the Hailo-8 finds a prop in it, the result crosses two processes and lands in the filter — and by then the vehicle has moved. The fix is perfectly correct about where the vehicle was. Apply it to where the vehicle is and you have just pulled the estimate backwards in time, on purpose, at speed.

    fix age—
    moved since—
    applied on arrival—
    replayed—

    What the filter actually does

    It keeps a short buffer of its own recent states. When a measurement arrives carrying the instant it describes, the filter winds itself back to that instant, applies the correction there, and replays every motion input since — so the correction lands on the state it was actually about, and the vehicle's present position is a consequence rather than an override.

    This is the only reason a timestamp is worth anything. A message that says "the prop is here" is a rumour; a message that says "the prop was here, at this instant on the board's clock" is a measurement. The board stamps its own messages, which is what makes the rewind possible at all.

    Why it looks fine until it doesn't

    Hold the vehicle still and both tracks agree exactly — latency multiplied by zero speed is zero error. Every bench test, every dry run, every "we checked it in the lab" is that case. The bug ships because the condition that reveals it is the condition you cannot create indoors: moving, turning, and being corrected at the same time.

    Drag the yaw rate to zero and the two tracks separate along one line — bad, but at least honest. Put the turn back and the naive track leans into the corner, because a correction that is 180 ms stale during a 18 °/s turn is a correction about a heading 3.2° ago.

    A late measurement is not noise. It is a true statement, applied to the wrong moment.

    Retractions // kept on purpose

    The results we had to take back.

    The measurement ledger holds 67 entries and 581 rows of numbers. 12 of those entries say, in their own title, that they took an earlier result back; 6 more measured an idea and the answer was to leave it off. They are kept where anyone can read them, because a project that quietly deletes its wrong answers is a project you cannot check. The full ledger is here.

    Image enhancement made detection better

    gate detected in 30.4 % of frames → 1.2 % with it on

    Seventeen configurations across four props and three venues. Never once positive. The implementation was correct; the original A/B was confounded. Preprocessing is off, and a test keeps it off.

    The confidence-adaptive filter tracked good boxes more tightly

    selectivity 0.78× — it was doing the exact opposite

    The shipped filter followed doubtful detections more closely than confident ones. It had been live, and every test passed, because no test compared the two directions.

    Range-dependent gain cut jitter 16×

    a pooling artefact, not an effect

    One clip contributed 1,299 far-field samples at the lowest jitter and zero near-field ones. The trend was an artefact of how samples were pooled. The feature stays off.

    Thruster health was verified

    958 of 958 ESC frames read exactly 0 — with nothing plugged in

    The check counted messages arriving, and messages arrive whether or not a motor exists. A gate that cannot fail is not a gate.

    The depth loop works

    it has never once run closed

    The sign was inverted until it was found, and the sensor was not fitted when it was written. Two armed bench checks now gate every automatic move — including a plain forward command, because on this board every automatic primitive closes the depth loop.

    The language // how a machine is asked to do something

    A mission should read like an instruction, not a state machine.

    One action, thirty verbs, two vision primitives. The vision verbs never raise — they return where and how they finished, so a mission branches on reality instead of on an exception.

    # hold station on the target, then fire mid-hold — still correcting
res = mongla.vision.align(
    'hole', lat=0, yaw=0,     # centre it: signed px from frame centre
    fwd=62, fwd_mode='area',  # and hold this standoff
    hold=4.0, fire=[1, 2],    # two shots, spaced, each gated on a NEW frame
    lock_on=True, brake=False,
)

if not res.saw_target:        # never read x_px before this — NaN < n is silently False
    return search_pattern(mongla)
if res.x_px > 0:              # it ended right of centre, so recover right
    mongla.move_right(duration=0.6)

    On the board this runs as streamed MANUAL_CONTROL in STABILIZE; an axis that would move the depth setpoint is refused, and comes back as FAILED rather than an exception.

    The comment on the eighth line is the whole philosophy compressed: a result that is absent must never be mistaken for a result that is zero. The full verb table is in command-reference.md; what a vision verb hands back is in vision-results.md.

    A mission that cannot say what went wrong is a mission that cannot recover from it.
    the language

    The last idea // and the one nobody writes down

    The vehicle has no present tense. It has a stack of pasts, and it steers on all of them.

    Every number the controller uses is a statement about a moment that has already gone, and each one is a different moment. The attitude is two milliseconds old. The depth is ten. The detection is eighteen at best, and during a gap it can be four seconds. Nothing in the code declares this — there is no variable called now to be wrong. The vehicle simply acts, at 500 Hz, on a picture assembled from clocks that never agreed.

    Why this is not a latency chart

    Latency is a number you quote once. This is the thing latency does when you leave it running: the ages never line up, so every control tick blends a two-millisecond attitude with a detection from a fifth of a second ago and acts as though both describe the same world. Most of the time that is fine, because the world did not change much in that fifth of a second. The interesting question is the one the picture asks by itself — how wide is it allowed to get before "fine" stops being true?

    That question has a shipped answer, and it was measured rather than chosen: VISION_FRESH_ZERO_S is 0.20 s, which covers 56.3 % of the real gaps, and the lock ladder behind it was sized against the same 71 dropouts. The rungs exist because the band in this figure gets wide.

    What surprised us about it

    Drag the figure into a p90 gap and count the ticks: the board runs several hundred control cycles on a detection that has not been refreshed once. The reflexes are fine — they never needed the camera — but every decision layered on top is being made about a world that has moved on, and the estimate of how far it moved is itself an estimate.

    This is the honest reason a vehicle can be excellent at every individual number on this site and still lose a run. Nothing failed. The parts simply did not agree about when it was.

    There is no instant in this system where everything is true at once. Now is a band, and the engineering is in how wide you let it get.
