Mongla · an autonomy stack for autonomous underwater vehicles

Machines that have to work when nobody is watching.

Underwater there is no radio, no GPS, and no second chance to explain yourself. A vehicle either understood what it was looking at, or it did not — and it will not tell you which. This is the software that has to be right anyway.

Act II — owning the whole stack

The ceiling was never the hardware. It was that the fastest, most safety-critical loop belonged to somebody else.

An autonomous vehicle has to answer two kinds of question. Which way is up, and how deep am I? — hundreds of times a second, because water pushes back. And what is around me, and what should I do about it? — which takes a camera, a neural network, and time. Put both on one computer and the slow question delays the fast one. So they were split: reflexes onto a board we wrote the firmware for, thinking onto a Pi with dedicated inference silicon, one cable between them carrying intent rather than reflexes.

The machine, layer by layer

Each layer trusts the one beneath it to be faster than the problem it is solving. That is the whole architecture in one sentence.

What the water did to our conclusions

More than half of this project's measurement log is retractions: things we believed, tested properly, and withdrew. They are kept deliberately, because a result you cannot reproduce is worth more as a warning than as a number.

Brightening the image before the detector

+42 % recall30.4 % → 1.2 %

The most natural idea in underwater vision. Measured across 17 configurations, four props and three venues: never once positive. On the gate it destroyed 95 % of detections. It ships disabled, and a test keeps it disabled.

Softening the gain as a target fills the frame

16× improvement1.56×

The 16× was an artefact of pooling clips: one clip contributed 1 299 samples and manufactured the trend. Per-clip, the effect is between 0.6 and 3.0. The feature stays off.

Trusting confident detections more

shipping backwards0.78× → 5.37×

The filter had been weighting doubtful boxes more heavily than confident ones for its entire life. Nothing failed. It simply followed the wrong boxes, politely.

Asking whether a thruster is spinning

958 / 958 = 0

Every ESC frame arrived valid, decodable, and exactly zero — with no thrusters attached at all. A message count can never prove a motor is alive.

The depth controller

never closed

The loop that holds depth has never run closed in water. It gates every automatic move. We know because the board publishes its own error — something a sealed autopilot would never have told us.

The arming guard

safe by 0.20 mfailed open by 6×

A stale fallback constant made a 0.90 m phantom sensor error look like 0.20 m. The vehicle armed. The fix was to read the value from the board instead of assuming it.

The same gate, in two kinds of water

Left, the pool you tune in. Right, the pool you compete in. The detector does not get to choose, and nothing in the pipeline warns you which one you are in.

Holding on when the detector blinks

Detections flicker. Measured over 71 real gaps at full frame rate, the median is 0.155 s and the ninetieth percentile 0.651 s. So the grip on a target is a ladder, and every rung is sized against that distribution rather than a guess:

  • Freshness — the command decays toward neutral while blind0.20 s · 56.3 %
  • Coast on the tracker's predicted box0.80 s · 91.5 %
  • Grace before the target counts as lost1.00 s
  • Kalman prediction1.50 s · 94.4 %
  • Track buffer — nothing in the archive exceeds it5.00 s · 100 %

Above all of it sits a feature anchor that once held a target through 175 consecutive frames in which the detector saw nothing at all.

When the vehicle can't do something, that's a pull request

The board's firmware, the ground station and the motor-controller tooling are a second author's work, in his own repositories. Nothing is vendored. Every change we need is asked for, with the evidence attached.

The motors couldn't say they were present

The firmware computed it and dropped the value one line before the wire, so thruster health could only ever answer "unknown". We read their source, found the line, and sent it back as a one-line ask.

The board had nowhere to put a velocity

We had just turned the downward camera into a bottom-tracking velocity sensor, good to a centimetre over 30 cm — and there was no message to send it in.

The kill switch meant two things

"Power is live" and "I cannot hear the power board" arrived identically, and nothing on either side refused to arm a dead hull. One line, using their own rule.

Nine such asks are written down, each reproducible before it was sent. A handful of constants are shared between the repositories and frozen: neither side may move one alone, and a test reads the firmware's own headers to prove ours have not drifted.

How you ask it to move

A mission is a Python file. The object it is handed is the whole vehicle — and it is written to be read at a poolside at midnight.

# the gate task, as it actually ships
def run(mongla, log=None):
    mongla.mission_reset()                     # clear old state, re-zero depth at the surface
    mongla.use_budget(900, reserve_s=45)       # ration the run; the clock starts on arm

    try:
        mongla.arm()
        mongla.set_depth(-0.8)

        with mongla.task('gate', deadline_s=120):
            if mongla.vision.align('gate', yaw=0, lat=0, gain=30, duration=60):
                mongla.vision.move('gate', fwd=None)      # drive through it
    except TaskAbandoned:
        mongla.note('gate', 'ran out of time', success=False)
    finally:
        mongla.surface()
        mongla.disarm()

Three ideas hold that up. Two vision verbs, not nine — centre on it, or drive to it; neither raises, and both report where the target was, so a mission recovers instead of crashing. Effort is not points — a run is a budget, and each task is asked whether it is still worth attempting. Give up cleanly — a deadline cancels the goal in flight and hands the mission its fallback, rather than overrunning into the next task.

Act III — open

Nothing on this platform has been in water. The honest list of what stands between here and there:

  • The board's depth loop has never run closedtwo bench checks, in water
  • Thruster health cannot tell eight healthy motors from noneone-line firmware merge
  • Moves by measured distance are refusedfirmware pull request
  • The board cannot yet accept a velocity measurementfirmware pull request
  • The competition radio cannot reach through an aluminium hullexternal antenna
  • No mission has been flown on this platformwater time

None of that is hidden, because a map that promotes bench results to flight results is how a team finds out at the venue.

I keep going on hope rather than optimism. Hope is the decision to keep working on the better version of a thing while the current version is still broken. Pushing a technical limit is the most concrete form of that I know: every measurement that came out worse than expected is a small argument that the world is knowable, and that the next version can be better than this one.

The next act is open — the machine is still being built.
