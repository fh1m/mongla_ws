Mongla // an autonomy stack for autonomous underwater vehicles

Machines that have to work when nobody is watching.

Act one // where it learned

It learned most of what it knows from losing to water.

Mongla began as the autonomy software for a university AUV programme. It flew on that programme's vehicles. The vehicles, the team and the name stayed there; the software, and everything it had been taught, came here.

2023

Before us — the team places 2nd

The programme's vehicle takes 2nd at RoboSub on a Pixhawk, ArduSub and a Jetson. This is the team's result, a year before the author joined; it is here as the ground he started on.

2024–25

Detection, tracking, and the slow discovery that most published underwater-vision advice does not survive being measured on your own footage.

2025

RoboSub — 8th place

The last campaign on the old stack. It worked, and it had a ceiling: our control ran at 20 Hz, steering a box we were not allowed inside.

2026

The stack leaves the lab

In September the author left the university, on principle. Mongla continues independently, with the firmware and hardware lead as its only co-author.

Act two // opening the box

We stopped renting the inner loop.

The old stack asked a closed autopilot to hold an attitude, at whatever rate our companion computer could manage. The ceiling was not our code — it was the boundary. So the boundary moved: reflexes went onto a board we write the firmware for, and the companion was left to do nothing but think.

What actually crosses the cable

If the reflexes live on the board, the Pi should have almost nothing to say — and it doesn't. Here is the whole of move_forward, the most ordinary thing the vehicle is ever asked to do, exactly as it leaves the Pi. Read it left to right, the way the board does.

The first ten bytes are the envelope: where a frame starts, how long it is, who sent it, what kind of message it is. The part that means anything is three numbers — a type, a duration, a speed — and one tag that says “this is ours”. There is no thrust in it. No motor, no angle, no depth. Which thrusters spin, how hard, and how the hull holds its heading for those three seconds is the board's business; the Pi only said what it wanted.

Play with it // the loop, in your hands

What a control loop is, if you have never written one.

A controller is a machine that answers one question, over and over: I am here, I want to be there — how hard do I push? Below is a toy hull with one axis of heading. Turn the three knobs and watch it fail in the three classic ways. Nothing here is our vehicle: it is a simulation with made-up numbers, so that the shapes are honest even though the values are not.

overshoot—
settled—
left over—
unanswered for—

Four things worth meeting by hand. P alone overshoots and rings: nothing is braking it, so at the moment it is finally pointing the right way it is still turning. Add D and the ringing dies, because it now pushes back against its own approach speed. At three seconds a current starts pushing, and the PD controller parks a degree or two short of the target for ever — it is pushing exactly hard enough to hold station and not hard enough to close the last degree. Only I fixes that, by remembering what is still missing. Then move the loop rate: the gains do not change, but the window in which the vehicle is on its own does. That is the number in the fourth readout.

On this vehicle that last knob is not ours to turn from the Pi. It is the board's, and it is pinned at 500 Hz on a core nothing else is allowed on — which is the whole argument of the section above.

Where the design is strong

Four tunnels and one axial unit buy five of six degrees of freedom — surge, sway, heave, pitch, yaw — with no vectoring linkage, no moving nozzles and nothing protruding into the flow. Tunnels also deliver full authority at zero forward speed, which is precisely the regime a task is won in: holding still in front of a hole. Bilateral symmetry is exact to ±88.05 mm, so there is no built-in turn to trim away. And the pitch couple is 48 % wider than the yaw couple (519 mm against 350 mm), which puts the stronger moment on the axis that has to resist a nose-down trim.

Where it costs

The four bores total 221.7 cm² of open aperture on a hull whose frontal area is 160.9 cm² — the holes come to 1.38× the frontal area. Open tunnels are cavities in cruise: they are the price of station-keeping authority, and it is paid continuously while transiting. At a fineness of 3.99 the body is already blunter than the drag optimum, so the two choices compound. The couples are also arguably the wrong way round — yaw is the axis an AUV actually uses, to hold heading against current and to line up on a target, and yaw got the narrower 350 mm couple. Swapping the tunnel pairs would favour the axis doing the work. And with every thruster on the centreplane, roll has no actuator at all: it has to be passively stable, which promotes the vertical placement of ballast from a detail to a load-bearing decision.

What this drawing cannot tell you

All of the above is geometry, and geometry is half a hull. These are unmeasured, and will not be guessed at here:

  • Net buoyancy — whether it floats at allneeds material densities
  • Centre of buoyancy above centre of mass — the whole roll argumentneeds a mass model
  • Drag coefficient, and so the power to cruiseneeds a tow test or CFD
  • Thrust per tunnel, and so whether five are enoughneeds a bollard test
  • Whether the modelled motors are the ones fitted3 of 5 populated in CAD

A shell mesh cannot answer the buoyancy question, and it would be easy to pretend otherwise: the enclosed volume of this model is 1.77 L, which is the surface and not the displacement. Using it would repeat a mistake this project has already made once, and written down. The geometry here is read straight out of the CAD by pack_hull.py, which is also what builds the model above.

The suite // three repositories, one wire

Mongla is the soul. It does not own the body.

Four repositories, two authors, and a rule that is easy to state and hard to keep: we never commit to theirs, they never commit to ours. Anything missing at the low level is a pull request, not a workaround on our side.

Hengla — what runs on the board

Core 1 is the flight loop and nothing else is permitted to run there: read the IMU, decide what each axis should do, split that across eight thrusters, listen to what came back, repeat two milliseconds later. Core 0 carries everything that can afford to wait — MAVLink at 100 Hz, LoRa and the SD card at 20, the display at 30. The RP2350 speaks bidirectional DShot, so an ESC reports the RPM it actually reached rather than the one it was asked for.

That last detail is the one worth pausing on. On the old stack the motors never answered: you asked for thrust and the honest answer to "is thruster three actually spinning?" was nobody knows. Telemetry back from the ESC turns an open question into a measurement — which is exactly how we learned that 958 of 958 recorded ESC frames read zero with nothing plugged in, and that counting messages is not a health check.

Bondor — the operator's side

An Electron and React desktop application built specifically for this board: teleop, a SROT_MOVE console, parameters, calibration, motor test, PID tuning and autotune, black-box analysis and a configurable joystick tab. It connects over USB serial at 115200 — opened with DTR and RTS de-asserted, deliberately, so that attaching a ground station does not reset the flight controller — or over UDP, or receive-only over LoRa at 433 MHz, 250 kHz bandwidth, SF7, CR 4:5.

Three copies of the wire contract exist on purpose, because there are no submodules across the suite: the LoRa struct, the vendored MAVLink dialect, and a hand-mirrored TypeScript copy of the message ids. The firmware is the source of truth for all three, and a mismatch does not raise an error — it fails CRC silently and looks exactly like being out of radio range. On our side the same hazard is answered by a test that reads the firmware's own headers and fails if our constants have drifted from them.

Bondor is not screenshotted here. It is the firmware team's application, it needs the board attached to show anything true, and a picture of it running against nothing would be a picture of nothing. The operator surface further down this page is ours, and it is real.

Continuity // when the detector blinks

The target does not leave. The detector does.

A neural detector on real underwater footage drops the box constantly — a reflection, a bubble, a bad angle. If control trusts only the detector, the vehicle loses a lock it never actually lost. So there is a ladder, and each rung is allowed to be wrong for a measured length of time.

rungholds the lock whenmeasured
Live detectionThe box is there this frame53.9 Hz
Tracker coastA frame or two is missing0.8 s of authority, decayed by true age
Geometric anchorThe detector is gone entirelyheld 175 consecutive frames
RefuseNothing above is honest any morereturns LOST, never a guess

The third row is the one worth staring at. Through a gap of 175 frames the detector produced nothing at all, and the vehicle still knew where the target was — because the anchor matches image structure rather than asking the network again. The fourth row matters as much: the ladder ends in a refusal, not in a confident invention. The rungs and their timeouts are described in detection-continuity.md.

Play with it // the sensor we could not buy

A camera pointed at the floor is a speedometer, if you know one number.

What we have is a camera pointed down, and the fact that the floor slides past it — but the same pixel motion means a different speed depending on how far away the floor is.

Three real slides of 30 cm on the bench rig, downward camera at 0.72 m lens-to-floor, f = 513.94 px. The honest part is the third column: the height the measurement implies, recovered from the slide it got wrong.

The simulator // 20,649 lines, thirteen courses, one purpose

A place to be wrong that costs nothing.

A second colcon workspace inside this repository: Gazebo, both competition pools, thirteen courses, both cameras, ground truth, and an operator lab. It is not a demo. It is where every recovery path in the stack got run for the first time, because a pool day is four hours long and a bug is cheaper here.

Each of those rows started as a thing that looked fine. The pattern repeats so exactly that it became a rule: the simulator's job is not to look right, it is to be wrong in the same places the water is. A static prop generates contact and cannot be pushed. A cylinder and a box return no collision at all in one physics engine, so the vehicle drove through every gate leg and slalom pipe for months. Fog does not reach camera sensors, so thirteen courses shared one clarity while claiming six.

The archive higher up this page came out of here — 4,925 labelled frames, 27 classes, both competitions — and so did the honest finding that two of those sessions detect nothing under the current weights.

Retractions // kept on purpose

The results we had to take back.

The measurement ledger holds 50 entries and 390 rows of numbers. 8 of those entries say, in their own title, that they took an earlier result back; 5 more measured an idea and the answer was to leave it off. They are kept where anyone can read them, because a project that quietly deletes its wrong answers is a project you cannot check. The full ledger is here.

Image enhancement made detection better

gate detected in 30.4 % of frames → 1.2 % with it on

Seventeen configurations across four props and three venues. Never once positive. The implementation was correct; the original A/B was confounded. Preprocessing is off, and a test keeps it off.

The confidence-adaptive filter tracked good boxes more tightly

selectivity 0.78× — it was doing the exact opposite

The shipped filter followed doubtful detections more closely than confident ones. It had been live, and every test passed, because no test compared the two directions.

Range-dependent gain cut jitter 16×

a pooling artefact, not an effect

One clip contributed 1,299 far-field samples at the lowest jitter and zero near-field ones. The trend was an artefact of how samples were pooled. The feature stays off.

Thruster health was verified

958 of 958 ESC frames read exactly 0 — with nothing plugged in

The check counted messages arriving, and messages arrive whether or not a motor exists. A gate that cannot fail is not a gate.

The depth loop works

it has never once run closed

The sign was inverted until it was found, and the sensor was not fitted when it was written. Two armed bench checks now gate every automatic move — including a plain forward command, because on this board every automatic primitive closes the depth loop.

The language // how a machine is asked to do something

A mission should read like an instruction, not a state machine.

One action, thirty verbs, two vision primitives. The vision verbs never raise — they return where and how they finished, so a mission branches on reality instead of on an exception.

# hold station on the target, then fire mid-hold — still correcting
res = mongla.vision.align(
    'hole', lat=0, yaw=0,     # centre it: signed px from frame centre
    fwd=62, fwd_mode='area',  # and hold this standoff
    hold=4.0, fire=[1, 2],    # two shots, spaced, each gated on a NEW frame
    lock_on=True, brake=False,
)

if not res.saw_target:        # never read x_px before this — NaN < n is silently False
    return search_pattern(mongla)
if res.x_px > 0:              # it ended right of centre, so recover right
    mongla.move_right(duration=0.6)

On the board this runs as streamed MANUAL_CONTROL in STABILIZE; an axis that would move the depth setpoint is refused, and comes back as FAILED rather than an exception.

The comment on the eighth line is the whole philosophy compressed: a result that is absent must never be mistaken for a result that is zero. The full verb table is in command-reference.md; what a vision verb hands back is in vision-results.md.

Shoulders // where the ideas came from, and what they changed

Borrowed convictions, and the line of code each one moved.

Not a reading list. Each of these is a specific idea, from a named source, that changed something concrete in this stack — and the change is named too.

Hotz

“Complex things eventually collapse under their own weight.”

The magic, he says, is more capability at no extra complexity — tinygrad as “the RISC of the machine learning stack.” What it changed: the hull viewer on this page has no dependencies and no build step, because the alternative was 600 KB of library to draw one mesh. The same reflex deleted a second stylesheet that had quietly become a second design system. Latent Space interview

Carmack

“The speed of light sucks.”

His latency work treats every stage of a pipeline as somewhere to claw back milliseconds, and he published his daily engineering notes for years on the conviction that work you will publish is work you do more honestly. What it changed: photon to detection is 18.0 ms and each stage of it is measured separately — and the camera became a mailbox rather than a queue, which took frame staleness from 396 ms to 16.9 ms. Latency Mitigation Strategies

Karpathy

Software 2.0 — the program compiled from data.

A neural network is a different kind of software: you do not write the program, you shape the dataset and the objective. What it changed: the detector is treated as a component with a measured operating envelope, not as an oracle — which is why the archive above exists, why thresholds are measured on real footage rather than borrowed, and why “image enhancement helps” was tested seventeen ways before being thrown out. Software 2.0

Keller

Every few years, design it again rather than patch it.

His argument is that the rewrite comes out both faster and simpler than the accreted thing it replaces, and that the reason teams do not do it is fear of a bad quarter. What it changed: the whole of this page's Act Two. The old stack placed 2nd and 8th and still had a ceiling, and the answer was not to tune it harder — it was to move the boundary and write the inner loop ourselves. On designing from scratch

Lattner

“Work backwards from the speed of light of hardware.”

A compiler exists so you need to know less about the machine; the mistake is working forwards from what you already have. What it changed: the perception path is sized against what a Hailo-8 can actually do — 80.9 Hz standalone — and the decode was kept in the quantised domain rather than converted to floats, which took it from 31.5 ms to 0.93 ms, bit-for-bit identical. Deep dive on Mojo

Rubin

“Inspiration comes first. You come next. The audience comes last.”

And: keep removing until it hurts, because that is usually where the soul of the thing is. What it changed: this page. The palette was not chosen — it was sampled out of the vehicle's own CAD materials, so the red here is the red the hull is painted. Every section that only restated another section was cut. The Creative Act

There is one more, and it is not a quote. Asked what makes an engineering write-up worth reading, the most common answer engineers give is: a candid view of the real trade-offs, the real implementation, and the failures. That is the permission this page took to put its retractions in the middle instead of the appendix. Ask HN: what makes an engineering blog great

Act three // open

Nothing here has been in water yet.

The honest list of what stands between this and a vehicle that swims:

  1. The board's depth loop has never run closed two armed bench checks, then waterwater
  2. Thruster health cannot tell eight healthy motors from none the firmware already computes it and drops it one line earlyone-line merge
  3. Moves by measured distance are refused no such primitive on the board; refused before dispatch, never fakedpull request
  4. The board cannot yet accept a velocity measurement we have one, good to 1.09 cm over 30 cm, and nowhere to send itpull request
  5. The competition radio cannot reach through an aluminium hull an external antenna, not a software fixhardware
  6. Three of five thrusters are populated in the CAD and the firmware's mixer is written for eighthardware
  7. No mission has been flown on this platform every number on this page says bench, footage, or boardwater

None of that is hidden, because a capability map that promotes bench results to flight results is how a team finds out at the venue. The live list is ROADMAP.md — one status file, so it cannot disagree with itself.

I left on principle, and I am building this on hope rather than optimism. Hope is the decision to keep working on the better version of a thing while the current version is still broken. Pushing a technical limit is the most concrete form of that I know: every measurement that came back worse than expected is a small argument that the world is knowable, and that the next version can be better than this one.
